## Our KubeCon Talk

At KubeCon we shared a talk about a problem many platform teams run into once
their internal platform starts succeeding: scale changes everything. It is one
thing to build a useful platform product for a handful of teams. It is
something else entirely to support dozens or hundreds of teams without drowning
in support questions, feature requests, documentation gaps, and ad hoc
prioritization.

The talk used a slightly dramatic analogy from the battlefield of Gondor, but
the point was familiar enough to anyone working in a large organization. When
questions, requests, and incidents come from every direction, the challenge is
no longer just building good technical capabilities. The challenge becomes
creating systems around the platform that let the team scale its impact.

eline spoke from our experience building Nova at Telenor, where the platform
supports the equivalent of around 120 teams. Jakub complemented that with
practical advice for teams that are earlier in their platform journey and want
to show clear value from platform engineering investments.

## Talk Overview

The core message of the session was that platform-as-a-product is the right
foundation, but product thinking alone is not enough when adoption grows. User
interviews, roadmaps, and best practices still matter, but eventually the
platform team needs mechanisms that scale communication, support, feedback, and
continuous improvement.

From our side, that meant treating each platform capability as a true product,
not just a technical building block. A platform product needs documentation,
support expectations, reliability commitments, and a clear story that helps
users understand what the platform offers and how to use it.

From Jakub's side, the message was equally pragmatic: if you are not yet at the
stage where you have dedicated insight tooling or advanced analytics, you can
still start by identifying repetitive developer pain, measuring wasted effort,
and applying proven platform capabilities to remove it. That is often the
fastest way to demonstrate return on investment.

## Key Takeaways

### Build a Community

One of the most practical pieces of advice was also one of the simplest: not
every platform question needs to be answered by the platform team.

If all support flows directly to the people building the platform, scale becomes
painful very quickly. A shared support channel can become much more than a queue
for incoming requests. Over time it can become a self-service community where
users answer each other's questions, share patterns, and surface better ways of
using the platform.

This helps in several ways. It reduces the support burden on the team, it
spreads knowledge between users, and it gives the platform team a better view
into how the platform is actually being consumed in practice. Some of the most
useful insights come from watching how users help each other.

### Focus on Communication

If users do not know what your platform offers, they will ask. When that
happens at scale, communication becomes part of the product surface.

That is why we emphasized the importance of having a polished platform website,
not just a collection of technical documents. A good platform front page should
make the available products visible, explain what each one is for, and help
users navigate quickly to the right capability.

This matters because a platform product is broader than an API, a cluster, or a
pipeline template. It also includes documentation, support, service levels, and
operational clarity. Communicating that clearly makes the platform easier to
adopt and easier to trust.

### Capture and Prioritize Insights

Platform teams receive ideas constantly: in Slack, in email, in hallway
conversations, and from their own engineers. If these insights are not captured
systematically, they are easy to lose and difficult to prioritize later.

We recommended creating a dedicated place for suggestions and requests, ideally
something that supports more than a simple issue backlog. A good insight system
lets users see what has already been suggested, upvote ideas they care about,
add context, and signal urgency. That gives the platform team stronger input
when deciding what to build next.

The quality of the discussion matters too. When users comment on each other's
requests, the team gets a better understanding of the real problem behind the
request rather than just the first proposed solution.

### Spend Time to Save Time

One of the strongest themes in the talk was that time savings rarely happen by
accident. Teams need to deliberately invest time in improvements that will pay
back later.

For us, one example of that is Innovation Days: two focused days every couple
of months where people can work on ideas that do not fit neatly into the normal
delivery cycle. These projects can target internal friction, user pain points,
or new ideas that deserve fast validation.

That gives us a second learning track alongside regular product delivery. If an
idea proves useful, it can move into the normal development cycle with evidence
behind it rather than remaining a vague improvement wish.

## A Practical Example: AI Support in Slack

We used one concrete example in the talk to show what "invest to save time" can
look like in practice. During one of our Innovation Days, engineers built a
prototype for answering support questions in Slack with AI.

The design was intentionally pragmatic. A smaller model first evaluates whether
an incoming message is actually relevant to Nova and whether it should be
answered at all. If the answer is yes, the request is forwarded to a larger
model with access to our documentation and historical support conversations.

That setup matters because not every message in a support channel deserves a
response. By filtering first and answering second, we reduce noise while still
making accumulated platform knowledge easier to access. The result is a support
experience that can scale better without losing grounding in real platform
documentation and prior answers.

If you want more detail on that implementation, we have already written a
separate post on the [AI Support Agent](https://cloud.telenor.no/blog/ai-support-agent).

## Starting Smaller and Showing Value

Jakub's part of the talk focused on how platform teams can begin this journey
without needing a fully mature platform organization from day one.

One useful framing comes from the idea of golden paths across domains like
DevOps, MLOps, and DataOps. These domains often contain repetitive work that is
valuable but not differentiating. When platform teams remove that repetition,
they create measurable value for product teams.

A concrete example from the talk was resource requests and limits in Kubernetes.
If developers repeatedly spend time tuning these manually, that is a good
candidate for platform improvement. Technologies like Vertical Pod Autoscaler
and in-place pod resizing can reduce wasted effort and help teams right-size
workloads automatically.

The same logic applies to incident analysis. If you spend time categorizing
incidents and find that a significant percentage come from predictable resource
problems, that becomes both a product opportunity and a prevention opportunity.
Instead of only handling the incidents, the platform team can remove a class of
problems entirely.

## Culture Scales the Platform Too

Tooling alone does not solve the scaling problem. A large part of platform
engineering is cultural: creating clarity, enabling collaboration, and building
psychological safety so people can improve the platform together.

That starts with communicating mission, vision, and product strategy clearly.
Users need to understand where the platform is going and what problems it is
trying to solve. It also means inviting people into improvement work, whether
through innovation initiatives, feedback loops, or cross-functional
collaboration around pain points.

The talk also touched on servant leadership as a useful mindset for platform
teams. A strong platform team does not only provide tooling. It creates the
conditions for others to move faster, make safer decisions, and contribute to
continuous improvement across the organization.

## What Success Looks Like

To ground the talk in more than principles, eline shared some of the survey
signals we use to understand how Nova is perceived internally. At the time of
the talk:

- 87% of respondents said they were happy with Nova
- 88% felt the platform was keeping up with technology
- 95% said they trusted Nova security
- 75% gave positive feedback on our documentation

That last number was especially useful because it showed both progress and a
clear area for improvement. Good documentation is essential when a platform
team wants to scale without turning every task into a support conversation.

## Reflections

### From Jakub

One of the strongest takeaways from Jakub's contribution was that platform
teams do not need to wait for perfect maturity before they start acting like a
product organization. Even simple analysis of recurring incidents or repetitive
developer work can uncover opportunities with very visible impact.

That is an important reminder because platform engineering can sometimes be
presented as if it requires a large operating model, a full product function,
and extensive tooling from the beginning. In practice, it often starts with
identifying one painful repeated task, solving it well, and making the value
visible.

### From Eline

Eline's message was that scaling a platform is as much about communication and
organizational design as it is about technical excellence. A platform team can
build excellent capabilities and still struggle if users do not know what
exists, how to get help, or how to influence priorities.

The strongest platform products tend to be the ones that create clarity around
what they offer, make support and feedback visible, and continuously invest in
the systems that reduce future friction.

## Community Response

The response at KubeCon was strong, especially because the topic resonated with
teams dealing with the less glamorous side of platform growth: support load,
documentation debt, prioritization noise, and the challenge of proving value.

We had good conversations after the session around self-service support, how to
structure insight collection, and how to balance platform investment with
day-to-day delivery pressure. It was also clear that many teams are dealing
with the same transition from building useful capabilities to operating a
platform that can scale sustainably.

## Looking Ahead

KubeCon reinforced a direction we already believe in strongly: internal
platforms need both strong technical foundations and strong product systems
around them.

We will keep investing in the parts of the platform that reduce friction at
scale, especially communication, support, discoverability, and structured
feedback loops. The more the platform can help users help themselves, the more
time the team gets back to build the next layer of value.
